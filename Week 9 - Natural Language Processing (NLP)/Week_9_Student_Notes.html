1. From Raw Text to Numerical Data

The fundamental challenge of Natural Language Processing (NLP) is representation. Computers execute mathematical operations; they cannot natively ingest sentences like "I love AI." We must translate discrete text (separate, distinct words) into continuous mathematical vectors (decimal numbers the computer can multiply).

Concrete Example: Translating "I love AI"
Imagine our total vocabulary $V$ consists of only 4 words: {"I", "love", "AI", "Math"}.

One-Hot Encoding (Tokens)

  • "I"  → [1, 0, 0, 0]
  • "love" → [0, 1, 0, 0]
  • "AI"  → [0, 0, 1, 0]

Bag of Words (Sentence)

The sentence "I love AI" becomes a single sum vector:

Vector = [1, 1, 1, 0]

Notice how "Math" is 0 because it's absent. As vocabularies grow to 50,000+ words, these vectors become incredibly long and sparse (mostly zeros).

1.1 Traditional Preprocessing: Cleaning the Mess

Before advanced Deep Learning models ruled the field, raw text was cleaned heavily. A traditional pipeline involved:

  • Lowercasing: "The" and "the" become the identical string.
  • Stopword Removal: Eliminating high-frequency, low-meaning words ("and", "the", "is") to reduce noise.
  • Stemming/Lemmatization: Chopping prefixes/suffixes to find the root word. "Running", "Ran", and "Run" all map to "run".

1.2 Tokenization: Word, Character, and Subword (BPE)

We cannot parse an entire document at once. We break it into atomic units called Tokens (the smallest "pieces" of text the AI can see, such as words or sub-words).

  • Word-level Tokenization: Splitting by spaces (["I", "love", "AI"]). Limitation: massive vocabularies and inability to handle typos or completely new words.
  • Character-level Tokenization: ["I", " ", "l", "o", "v", "e", ...]. Limitation: characters carry almost no semantic meaning alone, making patterns very hard for the network to find.
  • Subword Tokenization (BPE): The modern standard used by models like GPT. Byte-Pair Encoding merges the most frequent pairs of characters. Common words stay whole ("love"), while rare words split into logical subwords ("un", "believ", "able"). This gracefully balances vocabulary size and semantic clarity.

1.3 Vocabulary and Classical Vectorization

Once tokens are defined, we map them to a Vocabulary (a dictionary lookup, e.g., "Apple" = ID 150). Next, how do we represent a full sentence?

  • Bag of Words (BoW): Represents a sentence purely by the count of each token. It destroys word order completely. "Dog bites man" and "Man bites dog" look mathematically identical in BoW!
  • TF-IDF (Term Frequency - Inverse Document Frequency): An upgrade to BoW. It mathematically penalizes words that appear too frequently across all documents (like "the") to highlight rare, meaningful words.
    $$TF-IDF(t, d, D) = tf(t, d) \cdot \log\left(\frac{N}{|d \in D : t \in d|}\right)$$
    Where $tf(t, d)$ is the frequency of term $t$ in document $d$, and the second term is the Inverse Document Frequency.
  • One-Hot Encoding: A vector of zeros with a single '1' at the word's ID. Extremely inefficient due to sparsity (when a vector is 99% zeros, which wastes memory).

1.4 Measuring Similarity: Cosine Similarity

Once text is converted to vectors, how do we know if two sentences are similar? We use the Cosine Similarity, which measures the cosine of the angle between two multi-dimensional vectors. Unlike Euclidean distance, it is agnostic to the magnitude (length) of the vectors, focusing purely on orientation.

$$\text{similarity} = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{A}\| \|\mathbf{B}\|} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i B_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} B_i^2}}$$

A result of 1.0 means the vectors are identical in direction; 0.0 means they are orthogonal (unrelated).

2. The Rise of Word Embeddings

The "Zero Meaning" Problem: In One-Hot Encoding, every word is treated as an isolated island. There is no concept of "similarity." Because every word gets its own unique ID and its own column in a matrix, the mathematical distance between "Pizza" and "Burger" is exactly the same as the distance between "Pizza" and "Quantum Physics." The computer has no way of knowing that two words are related.

Word Embeddings (like Word2Vec, GloVe, or Keras's layers.Embedding) solved this by mapping words to Dense Vectors (vectors packed with meaningful numbers instead of zeros). Instead of thousands of zeros, words are represented by a smaller list of floating-point numbers (e.g., a vector of size 300).

Analogy: The GPS for Meaning
Think of Word Embeddings like GPS coordinates on a giant map of human language.
  • One-Hot: Just a list of addresses with no map. "1 Main St" and "2 Main St" are just names; you don't know how close they are.
  • Embeddings: Coordinates (Latitude, Longitude). On this map, words like "King" and "Queen" are located in the same "Royal Neighborhood." Words like "Pizza" and "Burger" are in the "Fast Food District."
Concept: The model learns these coordinates by seeing which words appear in the same sentences across millions of books.

Word2Vec Learning Architectures:

  • CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words): Predicts a target word from its surrounding context. Fast and works well for frequent words.
  • Skip-gram: Predicts the surrounding context words given a single target word. Performs better with smaller datasets and rare words.

Both utilize a Softmax objective function to maximize the probability of predicting the correct word $w$ given context $c$:

$$P(w|c) = \frac{\exp({v'_w}^\top v_c)}{\sum_{w'=1}^V \exp({v'_{w'}}^\top v_c)}$$

These vectors are learned during training. The core intuition relies on distribution: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." If "Dog" and "Cat" frequently appear next to "Pet", "Leash", and "Cute", their embedding vectors will shift closer together in mathematical space!

Semantic Arithmetic: A famous consequence of well-trained embeddings is that mathematical operations mirror linguistic analogies:
Vector(King) - Vector(Man) + Vector(Woman) ≈ Vector(Queen)

In Keras, we add an Embedding layer as the very first layer of our text model. It acts as a learnable lookup table that maps each word ID to its dense vector:

from tensorflow.keras import layers, models

# vocab_size = total number of unique words in our vocabulary
# output_dim = how many dimensions each word is mapped to (64 is common)
embedding_layer = layers.Embedding(
    input_dim=10000,  # Vocabulary size: we track the 10,000 most common words
    output_dim=64,    # Each word becomes a 64-dimensional dense vector
    input_length=200  # Each input sequence has been padded to 200 words
)
Code Breakdown:
input_dim=10000 — the layer allocates one row in its weight matrix for each of the 10,000 words. This means the Embedding layer alone has 10,000 × 64 = 640,000 learnable parameters.
output_dim=64 — the size of each word's vector. Larger = richer representation, but slower to train.
input_length=200 — tells Keras our sequences are exactly 200 tokens long (after padding). The layer output shape will be [batch, 200, 64] — a small 2D matrix per review, not a flat number!

3. The Architecture for Text

3.1 The Sequence Problem: RNNs and LSTMs

To fix the "Bag of Words" problem of ignoring sequences, researchers developed Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). These models process text word-by-word sequentially, maintaining a "hidden state" (memory) of everything read so far.

The Vanishing Gradient Problem: Basic RNNs suffer catastrophically on long paragraphs. When updating weights (backpropagation through time), gradients diminish exponentially. By word 50, the network completely forgets word 1.

3.2 Advanced RNNs: LSTM and GRU

LSTMs (Long Short-Term Memory) introduced complex gates to "choose" what to remember and what to forget. The mathematical heart of an LSTM cell consists of three gates:

Forget Gate ($f_t$):
$f_t = \sigma(W_f \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f)$
Input Gate ($i_t$):
$i_t = \sigma(W_i \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i)$

These gates are used to update the Cell State ($C_t$), allowing information to persist across long sequences without being multiplied by small gradients repeatedly.

GRUs (Gated Recurrent Units) are a popular, simplified version of LSTMs that merge the forget and input gates into a single Update Gate ($z_t$) and a Reset Gate ($r_t$):

Update Gate ($z_t$):
$z_t = \sigma(W_z \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_z)$
Reset Gate ($r_t$):
$r_t = \sigma(W_r \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_r)$

GRUs are faster to train and have fewer parameters, making them ideal when training data or computation is limited.

3.2 1D Convolutions (TextCNN)

Surprisingly, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) from last week also work exceptionally well on text! If an image is a 2D grid, a sentence is a 1D sequence of word embeddings. We can slide a 1D filter across 3 words at a time. This allows the network to detect N-Grams (local word patterns) like "Not Good" which instantly flip sentiment.

3.3 A Glimpse of the Future: Attention

LSTMs are unparallelizable (you must process word 3 before word 4), making them painfully slow to train on GPUs. This sparked the quest for a new architecture. Modern NLP replaces sequences with the Attention Mechanism: algorithms that allow the network to look at all words in a sentence simultaneously and calculate which words relate strictly to each other. This is the foundation of GenAI and Transformers, which we cover next week!
