WEEK 11

AI Ethics, Bias, and Responsible AI

Course: Computer Systems Engineering
Module: Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Topic: Bias, Explainability, and AI Safety
Estimated Reading Time: 30 Minutes

Learning Outcomes
By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
1. Define algorithmic bias and its sources (Historical, Sampling, Measurement).
2. Explain the trade-off between Accuracy and Fairness.
3. Describe Explainable AI (XAI) techniques like SHAP and LIME.
4. Audit a model for simple demographic disparities.

[!GOAL]
Week 11 Goal:
We have spent 10 weeks learning how to build AI. Now we must ask should we build it? AI systems are not neutral; they reflect the data they are fed. In this module, we explore the critical responsibility of the AI Engineer. We will learn how to detect Bias in our models, how to make "Black Box" models Explainable, and how to ensure our systems are Safe and Robust.

1. The Myth of Neutrality

A common misconception is that "Math cannot be racist/sexist." While matrix multiplication is neutral, Data is historical. If you train a model on hiring data from the 1980s, the model will learn that "Men are managers" and "Women are secretaries," effectively automating past prejudices.

1.1 Types of Bias

  • Historical Bias: The world itself is biased. (e.g., CEO images are mostly men).

  • Representation Bias: The dataset underrepresents a group. (e.g., Facial recognition trained mostly on light skin fails on dark skin).

  • Measurement Bias: The label is a proxy. (e.g., Using "Arrests" as a proxy for "Crime" biases against over-policed neighborhoods).

Real World Fail: Amazon scrapped an AI recruiting tool because it penalized resumes containing the word "Women's" (e.g., "Women's Chess Club Captain"). It learned this because most successful hires in the past were men.

2. Fairness vs. Accuracy

Often, fixing bias reduces overall accuracy.

  • Group Fairness: Demographic groups should have similar metrics (e.g., Acceptance Rate).

  • Individual Fairness: Similar individuals should be treated similarly.

Consider a bank loan model.

  • Model A: 95% Accurate, but denies 80% of minority applicants.

  • Model B: 93% Accurate, but denies 40% of minority applicants (closer to the majority rate).

An ethical engineer might choose Model B.

3. Explainable AI (XAI)

Deep Learning models (especially CNNs and LLMs) are Black Boxes. We put data in, and get an answer out, but we don't know why. In high-stakes fields (Healthcare, Law, Finance), "The Computer Said So" is not acceptable.

3.1 Global vs Local Explanation

  • Global: How does the model work generally? (e.g., "It relies heavily on credit score").

  • Local: Why was this specific person denied? (e.g., "Because their debt-to-income ratio was > 40%").

3.2 SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)

Inspired by Game Theory. It calculates the contribution of each feature to the prediction.

  • Base Chance: 50%

  • Age = 25: +10%

  • Income = Low: -30%

  • Prediction: 30%

4. AI Safety & Security

4.1 Adversarial Attacks

You can trick a Neural Network by adding invisible noise to an image. To a human, it looks like a Panda. To the model, it is 99.9% confident it is a Gibbon.

  • Implication: Self-driving cars tricked by stickers on stop signs.

4.2 Data Poisoning

Attackers inject bad data into the training set to create a "Backdoor".

  • e.g., Teaching a spam filter that "Nigerian Prince" is safe.

5. Lab Preview: Bias Audit

In this week's lab, we will play the role of an AI Auditor.
1. Train: We will train a classifier on a "Credit Approval" dataset.
2. Audit: We will check the False Rejection Rate for Men vs. Women.
3. Fix: We will attempt to mitigate the bias using re-sampling or threshold adjustment.

6. Summary

  • Data is not Truth: Data is a snapshot of history, including its flaws.

  • Bias in, Bias Out: Models amplify existing inequalities.

  • Trust requires Explanation: XAI tools help us verify model reasoning.

  • Responsibility: As you build powerful tools, you inherit the duty to use them safely.
