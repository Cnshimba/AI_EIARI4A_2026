1. Supervised vs. Unsupervised Learning

In Weeks 2 and 3, every dataset we touched had a clear "correct answer" ($Y$). We knew the price of the house, or whether the email was spam. Our goal was simply to map Input $\rightarrow$ Output. This is Supervised Learning.

But what if we don't have labels? What if we just have a massive dump of raw customer data with no indication of "Good Customer" or "Bad Customer"?

This brings us to Unsupervised Learning. Here, the goal is not to predict a specific outcome, but to explore the data and find hidden structures. Notice how K-Means still relies on Linear Algebra (means, vectors) and Geometry (distance), even without labels—reinforcing concepts from Week 2 and Week 3. The feedback loop changes: there is no "Loss function" telling the model it was wrong. Instead, the model tries to group items that look similar.

Feature Supervised Learning Unsupervised Learning
Data Labeled ($X, Y$) Unlabeled ($X$ only)
Goal Predict outcomes Find hidden structures/patterns
Feedback "You were wrong" (Loss) "This looks similar to that"
Examples Regression, Classification Clustering, Dimensionality Reduction


Figure 1: Left: Supervised Learning matches shapes to known labels (Red/White). Right: Unsupervised Learning just sees dots and must find its own groups based on position.

Example 1: The Online Store

Imagine you run an e-commerce site.
- Supervised Approach: You use detailed past purchase history to predict exactly how much Rands a specific user will spend next month. You have a "Target".
- Unsupervised Approach: You just have a list of all user activity. You ask the AI to "Group similar users". It might discover that "Group A" buys diapers and beer (Parents), while "Group B" buys gaming laptops and energy drinks (Gamers). You didn't tell it these groups existed; it found them.

2. K-Means Clustering

Clustering is the task of dividing a dataset into groups (clusters) where data points in the same group are more similar to each other than to those in other groups.

The most popular and simple algorithm for this is K-Means. Its goal is to partition the data into K distinct clusters. By default, K-Means uses Euclidean distance to measure how close a point is to a centroid.

2.1 The Algorithm (The Dance of Centroids)

How does K-Means actually work? It is an iterative process that relies on finding the "center" of a group perfectly.


Figure 2: The K-Means Loop. It keeps cycling until the Centroids stop moving.

Example 2: The "Party Speaker" Analogy

Imagine a large party in a hall. You place 3 large speakers (Centroids) in random spots and play different music on each.

  1. Assign: Everyone walks to the speaker playing the music they like best.
  2. Update: You notice where people gathered, and you move the speaker to the exact center of that crowd to give them better sound.
  3. Repeat: Because the speaker moved, some people at the edge might now be closer to a different speaker, so they switch. You keep moving the speakers until everyone is happy and stops moving.

The Formal Steps:
1. Initialize: Pick K random points (Centroids).
2. Assign: Color every data point based on its nearest Centroid.
3. Update: Move the Centroid to the mathematical average (mean) of its cluster.
4. Repeat: Continue until convergence (Centroids stop moving).

Important Note: K-Means works best when clusters are roughly circular and similar in size. For complex shapes or varying cluster densities, other algorithms like DBSCAN or Hierarchical Clustering may be more appropriate.

2.2 Picking "K"

One major limitation of K-Means is that you must tell it how many clusters ($K$) to find before you start. But how do you know if your customers fall into 3 groups or 5 groups?

We often use the Elbow Method. We run K-Means for $K=2, 3, 4, 5...$ and measure the "tightness" of the clusters (how close points are to their center).


Figure 3: Finding the optimal K. We look for the "Elbow" where the rapid improvement levels off.

Visual Guide:
- Steep Drop: Adding a cluster helped a lot! (e.g., splitting a mixed group).
- Flat Line: Adding more clusters doesn't help much (just chopping up existing good groups).
- The Elbow: The perfect balance. In Figure 3, the arrow points to K=3.

3. Evaluating Clustering Results

Unlike supervised learning, unsupervised learning does not have labeled data, which means we cannot directly measure accuracy or precision. Instead, we evaluate how well data points are grouped using cluster evaluation metrics. These help determine whether clusters are compact (data points close together) and well-separated from other clusters.

3.1 Elbow Method

The Elbow Method determines the optimal number of clusters (k). We run K-Means with different values of k and compute the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS):

$$WCSS = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{x \in C_i} ||x - \mu_i||^2$$

Where $C_i$ is cluster $i$, $x$ is a data point, and $\mu_i$ is its centroid. As $k$ increases, WCSS decreases. The elbow point — where the rate of decrease sharply changes — indicates the optimal $k$.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

wcss = []
for k in range(1, 11):
    kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=k, random_state=42)
    kmeans.fit(X)
    wcss.append(kmeans.inertia_)

plt.plot(range(1, 11), wcss, marker='o')
plt.xlabel("Number of clusters (k)")
plt.ylabel("WCSS")
plt.title("Elbow Method")
plt.show()
🔍 Code Walkthrough — Elbow Method
Line 1: We import KMeans from scikit-learn and matplotlib for plotting.
Lines 3–6: We loop through values of k from 1 to 10. For each value, we create a new KMeans model, fit it to our data X, then store the inertia_ score (which is the WCSS) in the wcss list.
Lines 8–11: We plot k on the x-axis vs. WCSS on the y-axis. The elbow — the sharp bend in the curve — visually indicates the best k to use.

3.2 Silhouette Score

The Silhouette Score measures how well each data point fits within its assigned cluster by comparing:

  • a = average intra-cluster distance (distance to its own cluster members)
  • b = average distance to the nearest other cluster

$$s = \frac{b - a}{\max(a, b)}$$

ScoreMeaning
Close to 1Well-separated clusters
Around 0Overlapping clusters
NegativePossible misclassification
 
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=3, random_state=42)
labels = kmeans.fit_predict(X)

score = silhouette_score(X, labels)
print("Silhouette Score:", score)
🔍 Code Walkthrough — Silhouette Score
Lines 1–2: We import silhouette_score (the evaluator) and KMeans (the model).
Line 4: We build a KMeans model with 3 clusters and set random_state=42 for reproducibility.
Line 5: .fit_predict(X) is a shortcut — it trains the model AND immediately returns the cluster label (0, 1, or 2) for every row in X.
Lines 7–8: We pass data X and labels to silhouette_score(). A value close to 1.0 means the clusters are compact and well-separated.
Why Evaluation Metrics Matter
Cluster evaluation helps us: choose the correct number of clusters, measure cluster quality, and compare different algorithms. Without them, clustering results may look reasonable but may not represent meaningful patterns.

4. Distance Metrics

Clustering algorithms measure "closeness" using distance functions. Choosing the right one is critical.

MetricFormulaBest Used When
Euclidean Distance$d = \sqrt{\sum(p_i - q_i)^2}$Numerical data, features on similar scales
Cosine Similarity$\cos\theta = \frac{A \cdot B}{||A|| \cdot ||B||}$Text data, word counts, or normalized vectors
Key Insight: K-Means uses Euclidean distance by default. Because larger feature values dominate distance calculations, always apply StandardScaler to your data before clustering to ensure all features contribute equally.

5. Advanced Clustering Algorithms

K-Means works well when clusters are spherical and similar in size. For more complex data, consider these alternatives:

6. Association Rule Mining

Association Rule Mining is another key form of unsupervised learning, focused on discovering interesting relationships between variables in large datasets.

The most famous use case is Market Basket Analysis: discovering that customers who buy product A are also likely to buy product B.

Real-World Example
The classic rule: "People who buy diapers also tend to buy beer." This was discovered by a retail chain purely by mining their transaction database — no labels needed!

6.1 The Apriori Algorithm

The Apriori algorithm finds frequent itemsets (sets of products that appear together) and generates association rules from them. It uses two key thresholds:

  • Support: How often an itemset appears in the data. $Support(A) = \frac{\text{Transactions containing } A}{\text{Total Transactions}}$
  • Confidence: How often the rule is correct. $Confidence(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{Support(A \cup B)}{Support(A)}$

7. Practical Implementation

Here is a complete end-to-end example bringing together K-Means, the Elbow Method, Silhouette Score, and visualization using PCA.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

# 1. Generate synthetic data
X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=500, centers=4, random_state=42)

# 2. Scale the data
scaler = StandardScaler()
X_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X)

# 3. Elbow Method — find optimal K
wcss = []
for k in range(1, 11):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, random_state=42)
    km.fit(X_scaled)
    wcss.append(km.inertia_)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.plot(range(1, 11), wcss, marker='o')
plt.title('Elbow Method')
plt.xlabel('Number of clusters (k)')
plt.ylabel('WCSS')
plt.show()

# 4. Train final model with K=4
kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=4, random_state=42)
labels = kmeans.fit_predict(X_scaled)

# 5. Evaluate using Silhouette Score
score = silhouette_score(X_scaled, labels)
print(f"Silhouette Score: {score:.4f}")

# 6. Visualize using PCA (compress to 2D for plotting)
pca = PCA(n_components=2)
X_pca = pca.fit_transform(X_scaled)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
plt.scatter(X_pca[:, 0], X_pca[:, 1], c=labels, cmap='viridis', alpha=0.7)
plt.title('K-Means Clusters (Visualized via PCA)')
plt.xlabel('Principal Component 1')
plt.ylabel('Principal Component 2')
plt.colorbar(label='Cluster')
plt.show()
🔍 Code Walkthrough — Full Pipeline
Lines 1–7 (Imports): We import every tool needed — KMeans for clustering, make_blobs to generate data, silhouette_score to evaluate, PCA to compress dimensions for plotting, StandardScaler to normalise features, and matplotlib to draw charts.
Step 1 (Line 10): make_blobs() generates 500 artificial points arranged in 4 groups. The _ deliberately discards the true labels — we are pretending we do not know them, as in real unsupervised learning!
Step 2 (Lines 13–14): StandardScaler normalises the data so that no feature dominates the distance calculations by sheer scale. fit_transform() learns and applies the scaling in a single step.
Step 3 (Lines 17–25): We run the Elbow Loop. For each K from 1 to 10 we fit a model and record inertia_ (WCSS). The resulting line chart shows a clear "elbow" bend where improvement flattens — that is our optimal K.
Step 4 (Lines 28–29): We train the final model with K=4. fit_predict() trains and immediately returns a label (0–3) for every data point in one call.
Step 5 (Lines 32–33): silhouette_score() evaluates cluster quality. Values above 0.5 indicate reasonably well-separated clusters.
Step 6 (Lines 36–43): Because our data is multi-dimensional, we use PCA(n_components=2) to compress it to two axes. plt.scatter() then draws each point coloured by its assigned cluster — giving us the familiar cluster plot.
